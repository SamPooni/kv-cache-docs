LRFU Decayed-Frequency Scoring

Established prior art, worked through — not a new algorithm. How a block's score tracks sustained attention rather than mere recency.

This is LRFU, not a new algorithm — External literature

Why α = 0.05? The Half-Life Calculation
(1 - α)^n = 0.5
0.95^n = 0.5
n = log(0.5) / log(0.95)
n = 13.5 steps
At an illustrative 10 tokens/second per user, the half-life is about 1.35 seconds.

If a block receives no attention for 1.35 s, its score halves. Blocks that are consistently important hold high scores; blocks briefly touched and then ignored decay away.

α = 0.05 is the simulated optimum, not a derived constant. α = 0.2 was never validated and is withdrawn. In the sweep at 32 GiB, α is non-monotonic: 0.5→37.20, 0.3→38.84, 0.15→44.33, 0.05→55.96, 0.01→31.54, 0.003→25.47, 0.001→24.44. There is no convergence onto LFU as α→0. Simulated (provisional)

0.03 and 0.005 are uncalibrated design parameters

Neither threshold is derived, measured or fitted. They are placeholders chosen to make the three-tier decision legible, and they are stated here as assumptions so that a reader can argue with them rather than inherit them. They have been rescaled to α = 0.05; the earlier 0.10 / 0.02 pair belonged to the withdrawn α = 0.2 and does not carry over.

What they control. The keep threshold (0.03) sets the size of the HBM-resident working set: it is the score a block must hold to stay in HBM. The evict threshold (0.005) sets the floor below which a block is pushed off the DRAM tier to NVMe. The band between them is the demote zone, and its width sets how much traffic the middle tier absorbs.

Which way they push. Raising the keep threshold frees HBM sooner, at the cost of hit rate — more accesses fall through to a tier whose latency is assumed, not measured (32 / 64 / 121 GB/s → 164 / 82 / 43 µs for a 5.00 MiB block). Lowering it holds more blocks in HBM and raises hit rate at the cost of capacity. Raising the evict threshold reclaims DRAM faster and shortens metadata state, at the cost of a longer miss tail on NVMe; lowering it keeps more of the context warm and moves capacity pressure onto the DRAM tier. Widening the demote band trades HBM pressure for tier pressure; narrowing it makes the policy behave more like a two-tier keep/evict decision. What is not claimed is any particular hit rate at any setting — no single hit rate exists. Across budgets, policies and workloads the simulated range is 8.5–91.6%. Simulated (provisional)

Threshold tuning is not where the leverage is. At a 32 GiB budget the whole spread between scoring functions is smaller than one protocol change: letting identity-keyed policy state survive HBM eviction is worth +34.15 pts to LFU (15.56 → 49.71) and +24.85 pts to EMA α=0.01 (31.54 → 56.39), while EMA α=0.15 is unchanged (−0.21 pts). Tune the protocol before tuning α.

How they would be calibrated. Only against real attention traces. Replay attention distributions from a production 128 K workload, sweep the pair, and pick the point that meets a stated service objective at the smallest working set. The correct values almost certainly differ per model. Note also that the absolute scale of these thresholds is tied to α: an EMA lags its input, so a threshold of 0.03 is not "3% of attention". Changing α changes what these numbers mean — and α is non-monotonic, so there is no safe interpolation between settings.
