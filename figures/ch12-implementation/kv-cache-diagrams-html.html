The Memory Wall Problem

Figure: why long-context inference does not fit in GPU memory. A B200 has 192 GB of HBM, Llama-3 70B weights take 140 GB, which is 130.4 gibibytes, and one 128K-token KV cache takes 40.0 gibibytes at 320 kibibytes per token, so eight concurrent 128K contexts plus weights need about 489 GB — a capacity wall of 2.5 times the card. That arithmetic is analytical and stands. The second wall is not the latency ratio this figure used to claim. The 65 times figure — 13.0 microseconds of driver-mediated PCIe DMA divided by a 200 nanosecond hypothetical CXL load-store access — is retracted as a headline: the two are not the same operation, and no CXL hardware was measured. In its place is a measured result. On a DGX Spark GB10 on 19 August 2026, streaming copy achieved 236.5 gigabytes per second against a 273 gigabytes per second specification, 87 percent of spec, which retires the roughly 50 percent effective-bandwidth haircut used in earlier drafts. Fitting decode time as a two-term model, t equals a plus b times KV gibibytes, gave 100.3 plus 16.34 times KV for HuggingFace with SDPA unpaged at R squared 0.991, and 73.9 plus 17.58 times KV for vLLM 0.20.1 with FlashAttention-2 paged at R squared 0.945. Under that fit the inferred fixed-path bandwidth is 205.7 gigabytes per second and the inferred KV path 61.1, so the effective KV path is about 3.4 times more costly per byte than the fixed path — a model-derived ratio, not a direct byte-level hardware measurement. KV is read at roughly a quarter of achievable memory bandwidth. Kernel substitution alone did not eliminate the measured KV slope: two frameworks with different attention implementations and paged versus unpaged layout produced KV slopes within 8 percent, while inferred fixed-path bandwidth improved 34 percent between them. This does not generalise to all kernels, GPUs, models or decode regimes. Capacity was not the binding constraint in any configuration measured: everything fit in 128 gibibytes, yet throughput still fell from 150 tokens per second at batch 16 by 4K to 46 tokens per second at batch 16 by 16K, with memory to spare and nothing evicted. Adding a memory tier does not eliminate the KV memory wall; it changes its shape, from a capacity wall into a data-movement wall. B200 specifications quoted: 2,250 TFLOPS BF16 dense, 8 TB/s HBM3e, 192 GB capacity, 64 GB/s PCIe 5.0.

CXL Endpoint Architecture

Figure: the internal layout of one distributed endpoint, a CXL 3.0 Type-3 device. A CXL 3.0 protocol engine carries CXL.mem (HDM-D and HDM-DB), CXL.io (mailbox and config) and CXL.cache (coherency); a UCIe 1.1 die-to-die link at over 1 TB/s joins three chiplets — a memory controller (8 channels of DDR5-5600, 8 times 44.8 GB/s equals 358.4 GB/s), a compute chiplet (8 ARM A78 cores at 3 GHz with 8 MB shared L3) and a control and policy chiplet running the PLACE and MOVE stages: an LRFU scoring engine, a per-KV-head access tracker used as a scoring signal only, and a persistent block-state store keyed by block identity. There is no RoPE prefetch queue: RoPE is a rotation applied to Q and K at compute time and carries no information about which blocks are needed next, so the earlier RoPE prefetch queue is removed. The per-head tracker never evicts a head: every head of every layer is required for every token, so evicting a head produces wrong outputs, not a cache miss, and the placed unit is a (sequence, token block) of 16 tokens, 5.00 mebibytes. Scoring is LRFU, Lee et al., ACM SIGMETRICS 1999, established prior art, with alpha its documented LRU-to-LFU knob; alpha = 0.2 was never validated and the simulated optimum is near 0.05. The persistent block-state store is the one part of this chiplet with an empirical result behind it: letting identity-keyed policy state survive HBM eviction is worth plus 34.15 percentage points to LFU and plus 24.85 to EMA alpha 0.01 at a 32 gibibyte budget, simulated and provisional. Each endpoint holds 256 GB of DDR5 (1 TB across four) and 4 TB of NVMe (16 TB across four) at 80 W typical power. Tier latency is assumed, not measured: no CXL hardware was measured, and the former 200 nanosecond figure is demoted to an assumption inside a 32 to 121 gigabytes per second range, under which one 5.00 mebibyte block transfers in 164, 82 or 43 microseconds.

CXL 3.0 Coherency Protocol

Figure: the four coherency states CXL 3.0 maintains through the Back-Invalidate protocol. Invalid — the GPU cache is empty and the endpoint is authoritative. Shared — the GPU holds a read copy and the endpoint is still authoritative. Exclusive — the GPU may write and the endpoint copy is stale. Modified — the GPU holds dirty data and must write back. During prefill the GPU issues D2H writes that the endpoint applies to local DRAM; when the endpoint evicts to flash it issues a BI-Snoop that the GPU must acknowledge after writing back dirty data. Attention Mechanisms: MHA vs GQA vs MQA

Figure: three attention layouts compared by how many KV heads they keep. Multi-head attention keeps 64 KV heads at full memory cost; grouped-query attention, the highlighted middle option and the one used here, shares K and V across query heads for 8 KV heads and an 8 times memory saving; multi-query attention shares a single KV head at a quality tradeoff. Eight KV heads across 80 layers gives 640 per-layer-per-KV-head scoring streams — a scoring granularity, not an eviction one. There are not 640 eviction queues: every head of every layer is required for every token, so evicting a head produces wrong outputs, not a cache miss. All 640 streams are aggregated into one score per (sequence, token block) of 16 tokens, 5.00 mebibytes, and that block is what is placed, moved and evicted, with all 8 KV heads across all 80 layers travelling together. H2O and SnapKV evict head-token pairs, and the token axis is not optional. LRFU Block Eviction — established prior art, not a new algorithm

Figure: LRFU decayed-frequency scoring for block eviction, with a worked example. This is not a new algorithm. A decayed-frequency score is LRFU — Lee et al., "On the Existence of a Spectrum of Policies that Subsumes the LRU and LFU Policies", ACM SIGMETRICS 1999 — and alpha is its documented knob between LRU-like and LFU-like behaviour. LRU assumes recent access predicts future access, which attention can violate: a block at position 1,000 may go untouched until position 100,000 yet stay important. The update rule is score equals alpha times the new score plus one minus alpha times the old score. Alpha = 0.2 was never validated and is withdrawn; the simulated optimum is alpha about 0.05, giving a half-life of ln 0.5 over ln 0.95 equals 13.5 decode steps. Alpha is non-monotonic with an interior optimum: at a 32 gibibyte budget the simulated hit rates are 37.20 percent at alpha 0.5, 38.84 at 0.3, 44.33 at 0.15, 55.96 at 0.05, 31.54 at 0.01, 25.47 at 0.003 and 24.44 at 0.001, so a smaller alpha is not simply more stable, and there is no convergence onto LFU as alpha approaches zero. The evicted unit is a (sequence, token block) of 16 tokens, 5.00 mebibytes, never a head: every head of every layer is required for every token, so evicting a head produces wrong outputs, not a cache miss. Eviction priority is one minus the score, times recency decay, and higher priority is evicted sooner. Block A, an anchor block at position 1,024 last accessed 50 steps ago, scores 0.059 with recency decay 0.049, giving priority 0.046 — kept. Block B, a low-attention block at position 45,678 last accessed 2,000 steps ago, scores 0.02 with recency decay 0.865, giving priority 0.848 — evicted to NVMe, but its identity key and score persist and are restored on re-admission rather than reset. That persistence, not the scoring function, is the largest effect in the study: at 32 gibibytes it is worth plus 34.15 percentage points to LFU, from 15.56 to 49.71 percent, and plus 24.85 to EMA alpha 0.01, from 31.54 to 56.39 percent, while EMA alpha 0.15 is unchanged at minus 0.21 percentage points — larger than the whole spread between scoring functions, and a protocol result testable with no CXL hardware. The worked numbers are illustrative; the simulated figures are provisional, because a single data-structure defect in Revision 1 inverted four of five findings, and no single hit rate exists anywhere, the range being 8.5 to 91.6 percent across budgets, policies and workloads.

RoPE — What the Distance Bound Says, and Why It Cannot Drive Prefetch

Figure: a RoPE explainer. RoPE encodes position by rotating the query and key vectors in d over 2 two-dimensional subspaces, each with its own frequency, so the inner product is a sum over those pairs and does not decay monotonically. What the construction guarantees is an upper-bound envelope: the magnitude of the inner product of q_m and k_n is at most B of the absolute distance m minus n, with B non-increasing. It is never proportional to cosine of (m minus n) times theta; writing it that way converts a bound into a decay law, and a decay law reads like a prediction of access. Critically, RoPE is a rotation applied to Q and K at compute time. The rotation is a function of position alone, known in advance for every position, and identical whether a block is resident or evicted, so it carries no information about which blocks will be needed next and cannot drive prefetch. The prefetch rule this figure previously stated — on accessing position P, prefetch the window P minus W to P plus W, ordered by one over one plus distance over 100 — is withdrawn, together with the claim that RoPE prefetch contributes the last 4 percentage points of a 97 percent hit rate, from 93 to 97. The entire 72 to 80 to 87 to 93 to 97 ladder is retracted: it was never run as an ablation, and its increments were assumed additive and independent even though the mechanisms they decompose overlap. No single hit rate exists; the corrected simulation gives 8.5 to 91.6 percent across budgets, policies and workloads. The 200 nanosecond cost of a prefetch miss is also withdrawn: no CXL hardware was measured, and tier cost is an assumed 32, 64 or 121 gigabytes per second, under which a 5.00 mebibyte block is 164, 82 or 43 microseconds. The assumption that about 70 percent of attention mass falls within plus or minus W positions of the query remains an assumption — model-, layer-, head- and workload-dependent — and it is not something RoPE proves. A position-window prefetch remains an open idea, not a validated technique: it has not been shown to give a predictive advantage over the reuse-prediction approaches already shipping, such as CacheWise's tool-call-driven prediction, and it belongs in future work rather than in a list of contributions.

Prefill vs Decode Phase Characteristics

Figure: the two inference phases side by side. Prefill is compute-bound, writes sequentially, is write-only as it populates the cache, has high arithmetic intensity of about 100 FLOP per byte and processes the full sequence in parallel — so writes stream straight to CXL and no eviction is needed. Decode is memory-bound, does random reads plus one write per step, reads everything and appends one entry, has low arithmetic intensity of about 0.5 FLOP per byte and runs token by token — this is where placement and eviction matter. There is no RoPE prefetch in that strategy: RoPE is a rotation applied to Q and K at compute time and carries no information about which blocks are needed next, so the earlier "EMA eviction plus RoPE prefetch" strategy line is corrected to LRFU-scored block placement with persistent identity-keyed state. Note also the correctness constraint on the read pattern: under dense attention every block of an active sequence is read every step, so lossless residency requires every block that is read to be present, and treating attention sparsity as a substitute is a lossy relaxation that carries an accuracy-evaluation burden. The endpoint detects the phase from the read-to-write ratio and switches policy when reads exceed writes by 10 times. This is an analytical model; phase detection on real hardware is design, unvalidated, and no CXL hardware was measured.

The 2026 Landscape — the field specialised, it did not leave a gap

Figure: where KV-cache retention policy executes today, organised by the decision each layer optimises, and a table of the related work at each level. The earlier framing on this panel — that no publicly documented system did what this package proposes, so the combination and its placement were the contribution — is retired outright, not softened. In the serving framework: vLLM with PagedAttention, the allocation and paging baseline every offload layer sits on; SGLang with RadixAttention; NVIDIA Dynamo; LMCache, now a first-class cross-tier KV layer doing pinning, lookup, compression, batched movement, compute and I/O pipelining, cross-engine transfer and prefill-decode disaggregation; CacheWise, which predicts future KV reuse from agent and tool-call metadata; and Tutti, which removes the CPU from the GPU-to-NVMe KV I/O critical path. In the GPU model runtime: H2O, SnapKV, Scissorhands, PyramidKV, Quest, Ada-KV and HeadKV, which evict head-token pairs and allocate per-head budgets with attention scores in hand. On the host CPU: FlexGen, InfiniGen, LayerKV, KVPR and Mooncake, over a driver-mediated PCIe path. On the CXL device: Marvell Structera, Astera Labs Leo, Samsung CMM, PNM-KV, which selects which KV pages are needed before paying the movement cost from a processing-near-memory accelerator, and TraCT, a rack-scale prefix-aware CXL KV cache implemented on NVIDIA Dynamo with direct GPU-to-CXL operations. TraCT means CXL-native KV caching already ships, so describing this package as the sole complete CXL-native KV-cache system is false on its face. This package claims no novelty for its scoring function or its per-head structure either: a decayed-frequency score is LRFU, Lee et al., ACM SIGMETRICS 1999, established prior art with alpha as its documented LRU-to-LFU knob, and per-head signals are a scoring granularity only, since every head of every layer is required for every token and evicting a head produces wrong outputs rather than a cache miss. What is genuinely open is a unification, not an implementation: whether one KV-State Control Plane — SELECT, PLACE, MOVE, ATTEND, OBSERVE — should decide relevance, reuse, residency and movement jointly rather than leaving each to a separate subsystem tuned in isolation. Nobody has built or tested that, this package included. Its one empirical result sits inside PLACE and is simulated and provisional: letting identity-keyed policy state survive HBM eviction is worth plus 34.15 percentage points to LFU, from 15.56 to 49.71 percent, and plus 24.85 to EMA alpha 0.01, from 31.54 to 56.39 percent, at a 32 gibibyte budget, with EMA alpha 0.15 unchanged at minus 0.21 percentage points. It is a protocol result, testable with no CXL hardware. Treating it as validation of the whole pipeline would be a significant overclaim: it validates one box.

The “nobody has done this” framing is retired, not softened. An earlier draft argued the KV-cache-over-CXL landscape was uniformly unsophisticated with a wide-open gap. That does not survive contact with what has shipped: TraCT is already a CXL-native, rack-scale KV-cache system running on NVIDIA Dynamo.
